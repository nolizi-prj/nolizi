# Pumasi — Commercialization Foundations
### v0.1 — July 2026 — internal

**Purpose.** The whitepaper fixes what can never be sold. This document fixes how money is made within those limits. It contains only the load-bearing decisions — the ones that are nearly impossible to reverse once revenue, investors, or disputes arrive. Everything else (pricing, packaging, timing) is deliberately left out, free to change.

### 1. The rule

Sell everything except the commons: convenience, trust, and private use. Never access, never the code, never the ledger. Every future product must pass one test — *does it require the commons to be worse, slower, or less free for someone who pays nothing?* If yes, it does not ship, no matter the revenue attached.

### 2. The structure

Two entities from day one. A nonprofit foundation holds the commons: the repositories, the ledger, the trademark, and the certification standard. A for-profit company sells services on top and is the foundation's anchor member — with the same rights as any other member, and no more. The split is not decoration; it is what makes "forever" credible, and it must exist before the first dollar, because it cannot be created cleanly afterward.

Three asset placements decide everything. The trademark lives in the foundation; certification designations ("Certified Pumasi") are available to any provider on equal, published terms, and the company's own brand license is written and published at founding — the company's edge must come from being best, not from being only. The ledger lives in the foundation and is never an input to any commercial ranking. The certification standard lives in the foundation; the company sells assurance against it but does not control it.

The company incorporates as a public benefit corporation with the commons commitments written into its charter, so every investor buys in knowing the rule. The covenant is cheapest before the first check and impossible after the last one.

### 3. What we sell

**The anti-SaaS bundle.** One flat subscription for hosted, managed versions of the entire catalog — replacing the per-seat pile companies pay today. Boundary: self-hosting stays first-class forever, and the hosted version never carries an exclusive feature. The product is convenience, not capability.

**Certification and assurance.** Certified builds, signed provenance, patch response, compliance packaging, indemnification — accountability for agent-written software, which is the entire enterprise objection converted into a product. Boundary: the criteria are public, and the standard belongs to the foundation.

**Private operations.** Running the commons' machinery — quorum review, provenance, the test matrix — on code that will never be public, as a managed service. Boundary: the machinery itself is commons-built and therefore free; we sell its operation, never a license to it.

**Verification at large.** In time, every agent-written change everywhere will need heterogeneous review and signed provenance, most of it outside our commons. The commons is the living proof; verification is the product whose market is all agent code. Same boundary as above.

### 4. What funds the foundation

Memberships (labs, tool vendors, clouds — paying for association and a governance voice, never for outcomes), a platform fee on commissions sized to the verification cost each commission creates, and compute partnerships that attack the cost line directly. The foundation's budget and its sources are published, always.

### 5. Pricing philosophy

Price against the incumbent's bill, not against our cost — the structurally lower cost floor is the advantage, and the incumbent's invoice is the anchor. Flat and simple beats per-seat; per-seat is the sin that creates our wedge. And nothing the commons gives away free is ever metered by us, at any price.

### 6. The moat, honestly

Everything is forkable, so the company owns nothing exclusive — by design. Its durable advantages are exactly four: the certification relationship with the foundation, the provenance chain and the trust it carries, an operating cost no incumbent can match because agents run the operations, and proximity to the community that builds the catalog. When growth slows — and it will — pressure will come to manufacture a moat by enclosure: a license switch, an exclusive feature, a metered API. The answer is written here in advance, and it is no. The companies that broke this promise lost the community that was the moat.

### 7. Never

No open-core. No dual licensing. No license switch, ever — and to make that structural, contributions are accepted under inbound-equals-outbound terms (Apache-2.0 with a developer certificate of origin), never under an agreement that grants any entity relicensing power. No paywalled or metered reads. No selling of ledger placement or rank. No advertising. No hosted-exclusive features. Each of these is a known, named failure mode of open-source commercialization; this list exists so that no future meeting ever has to re-decide them.

### 8. Sequence

Revenue follows trust and never precedes it. The foundation earns first — memberships and commission fees — while the catalog gains standalone gravity; the company sells only once there is something the market already wants without persuasion. Monetizing an empty commons is the one mistake this document cannot otherwise prevent.
